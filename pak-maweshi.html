Overview

Pak Maweshi is an AgriTech marketplace I built with two schoolmates, Zarar Haroon and Ayaan Waqar, to fix a problem specific to Pakistan's livestock trade: farmers, especially women running small household herds, almost never sell directly to a buyer. Nearly every transaction runs through a local middleman who sets the price with no real transparency, and takes a cut the farmer has no way to negotiate around. On the health side, rural veterinary access is just as broken, most farmers have no reliable way to find a vet nearby at all.

I led the technical build from the founding-team stage onward: the Android app itself, the listing and messaging flow, and the Google Maps integration for the vet-finder feature. The app is deliberately pictorial-first and voice-guided in Urdu, because the target user often has low literacy and very little prior experience with apps of any kind. Every design decision, icon-driven navigation, minimal text, voice-over on every screen, traces back to that constraint.

Product & market

Pakistan's livestock sector runs almost entirely on informal, in-person trade. A farmer with animals or produce to sell typically has one option: wait for a middleman to pass through and name a price. Pak Maweshi's core flow replaces that with a direct listing: a farmer photographs an animal or product (the pilot started with livestock and has since expanded to smaller goods like eggs), sets a price and location, and a buyer anywhere on the platform can browse, message the seller directly, and arrange pickup, no middleman, no opaque pricing. A parallel "find a doctor" flow solves the second half of the problem, surfacing nearby veterinary contacts on Google Maps for farmers who'd otherwise have no way to locate one.

The business model pairs a for-profit marketplace with an explicit social mission. Revenue comes from transaction-adjacent services and, more recently, the Maweshi Madadgar initiative (below), while the core listing and vet-access features stay completely free, since adoption in this user segment lives or dies on there being zero barrier to trying it.

The scale of the underlying problem is part of what drew me to it: roughly 25 million rural farmers in Punjab depend on livestock for their livelihood, about 35% of the province's rural population, and 53% of employed women in these communities list livestock and poultry as their main economic activity. The total addressable market is every livestock-related rural area of Pakistan; the realistic near-term market is livestock-related rural Punjab specifically, with the pilot itself scoped to Sahiwal District.

Recognition & growth

The pilot ran in Sahiwal District, Punjab, the country's top livestock-producing region, and the app has since passed 450+ downloads on Google Play. Along the way it's picked up validation from a mix of institutional and competitive sources: incubation and $1,700 in funding through Spark Tank at Beacon House; the NSIT Young Innovators Award, with the cash prize presented directly by the Chief Minister of Punjab; and a place as a contender at MIT Solve 2024's Global Economic Prosperity Challenge, competing against ventures from around the world. On the institutional side, the app has been endorsed by the President/CEO of Zarai Taraqiati Bank Ltd (Pakistan's national agricultural development bank) and the Deputy Secretary of Punjab's Livestock Department, the kind of validation that's hard to get for a student-built app and matters a lot for something trying to operate in a heavily trust-based, in-person industry.

The traction has also spun off a second initiative: Maweshi Madadgar, a women-led livestock profit-sharing program funded by PKR 9M from Maple Leaf Capital, launched to extend the same core idea (cutting out the middleman) into a structured income-sharing model for women running household livestock operations.
